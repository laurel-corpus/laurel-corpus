# Original-language texts

*Part of the [Laurel corpus](../README.md) — see the [method index](../METHOD.md).*

17 works carry their original beside the English. Two things are done with them.

## Quantity, solved rather than looked up

Latin and Greek verse is measured in long and short syllables, not stressed and unstressed ones, and no
public database gives the quantities line by line. They are solved the way they are taught:

1. **Elide** a final vowel, or a final `-m`, before a word beginning with a vowel or `h-`.
2. **Syllabify** — one syllable per vowel or diphthong.
3. **Long by position** where two consonants follow the vowel (`x` and `z` count as two; a stop with `l`
   or `r` may count as one, so those are left open).
4. **Long by nature** where the alphabet says so. Greek marks much of its own quantity: eta and omega are
   long, epsilon and omicron short, the diphthongs long, and alpha, iota and upsilon open. Latin marks
   none, so its quantities come from step 5 alone, plus the positions.
5. **Fit the line to the metre.** For the hexameter: five feet each a dactyl or a spondee, and a sixth of
   two syllables. Only some arrangements give the right syllable count, and of those only some put a long
   where step 3 requires one. Where more than one still fits, prefer a dactylic fifth foot — true of about
   ninety-five hexameters in a hundred — and fewer spondees early.

**A line no arrangement fits is left unscanned**, and counted. Across the seven hexameter texts, 56,146 of
61,783 lines solve: 98–99% of the Latin, 80–88% of the Greek. The gap is real and has a cause — the Greek
texts are printed with accents and breathings this method reads through, and their proper names admit
quantities the rules cannot settle. The lyric metres of Catullus and Horace are not attempted at all.

## Lining a translation up with its original

The two columns in the reader have to stay level even though a translator takes 12 English lines to render
10 of Homer. Nothing is invented to make them do so:

1. Proper names are pulled from both texts. Greek is transliterated; Latin, Italian and the rest are folded
   through the spelling rules that carry a classical name into English (k→c, ph→f, th→t, ai/oi→e, case
   endings dropped), then cut to a stem.
2. A stem printed in both texts, and common in neither, proposes an **anchor** — this original line answers
   to that English one. Candidates far off the diagonal are dropped.
3. The anchors are reduced to the longest run that keeps both texts moving forward, then thinned so none
   sit within six lines of each other.
4. The first and last line of each section are anchored too, so the columns start and end together.

17 works, 548 sections, 16,875 anchors. **Between two anchors the lines are simply spaced evenly**, which
is guesswork of a mild kind, and the reader is told so on the page. Every anchor itself is a place where
the same name is printed in both texts.
