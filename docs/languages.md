# Original-language texts

*Part of the [Laurel corpus](../README.md). The [method index](../METHOD.md) lists the other documents,
and terms are defined in the [glossary](glossary.md).*

Seventeen works carry their original text beside the English. Two things are done with those originals.

## Quantity, solved rather than looked up

Latin and Greek verse is measured in long and short syllables rather than in stressed and unstressed
ones, and no public database gives the quantities line by line. They are therefore solved in the way
that they are taught:

1. **Elide** a final vowel, or a final *-m*, before a word beginning with a vowel or *h-*.
2. **Syllabify**, one syllable per vowel or diphthong.
3. **Long by position** where two consonants follow the vowel. *X* and *z* count as two consonants; a
   stop followed by *l* or *r* may count as one, so those cases are left open.
4. **Long by nature** where the alphabet says so. Greek marks much of its own quantity: eta and omega
   are long, epsilon and omicron are short, the diphthongs are long, and alpha, iota and upsilon are
   open. Latin marks none, so its quantities come from step 5 alone, together with the positions.
5. **Fit the line to the meter.** For the hexameter this means five feet, each a dactyl or a spondee,
   and a sixth foot of two syllables. Only some arrangements give the right syllable count, and of those
   only some put a long where step 3 requires one. Where more than one arrangement still fits, a
   dactylic fifth foot is preferred, which is true of about ninety-five hexameters in a hundred, and
   fewer spondees early in the line.

**A line that no arrangement fits is left unscanned**, and counted as such. Across the seven hexameter
texts, 56,146 of 61,783 lines solve: between 98 and 99% of the Latin, and between 80 and 88% of the
Greek. The gap is real, and it has a cause. The Greek texts are printed with accents and breathings that
this method reads through, and their proper names admit quantities that the rules cannot settle. The
lyric meters of Catullus and Horace are not attempted at all.

## Lining a translation up with its original

The two columns in the reader have to stay level even though a translator may take twelve English lines
to render ten of Homer. Nothing is invented to make them do so:

1. Proper names are pulled from both texts. Greek is transliterated; Latin, Italian and the rest are
   folded through the spelling rules that carry a classical name into English (*k* to *c*, *ph* to *f*,
   *th* to *t*, *ai* and *oi* to *e*, case endings dropped), then cut to a stem.
2. A stem printed in both texts, and common in neither, proposes an **anchor**: this line of the
   original answers to that line of the English. Candidates that fall far off the diagonal are dropped.
3. The anchors are reduced to the longest run that keeps both texts moving forward, then thinned so
   that none sit within six lines of one another.
4. The first and last line of each section are anchored as well, so that the columns start and end
   together.

Across the seventeen works there are 548 sections and 16,875 anchors. **Between two anchors the lines
are simply spaced evenly**, which is guesswork of a mild kind, and the reader is told so on the page.
Every anchor itself marks a place where the same name is printed in both texts.
